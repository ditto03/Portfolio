CASE-02 COMPLETED

Communication Barrier Nullifier Using Machine Learning

A real-time system that turns hand gestures into text and speech — and speech back into gestures — built to help bridge communication between the Deaf and hearing communities.

CategoryApplied ML / Computer Vision
TeamAjay Deepak J & Ditto D
GuideMs. Mehveesh Mariya, Dept. of CS
InstitutionSathyabama Institute of Science and Technology

Objective

Sign language is a full, expressive language — but most people who don't sign have no way to understand it without an interpreter. This project's goal was to close that gap in real time: recognize hand gestures through a webcam, convert them to text and spoken audio, and support the reverse direction too, so a hearing person's speech could be converted to text and eventually back to signs.

System architecture

The pipeline runs in five stages, from raw camera input to spoken output:

  1. Hand landmark detection — MediaPipe tracks 21 key points per hand (wrist, finger joints, fingertips) from the live webcam feed.
  2. Feature extraction & preprocessing — landmark coordinates are cleaned, normalized, and written to a structured dataset (CSV) for training.
  3. Model training — multiple classifiers are trained on the landmark features to recognize distinct gestures.
  4. Hybrid stacking — a meta-model combines the base classifiers' predictions to squeeze out extra accuracy.
  5. Output conversion — the recognized gesture is converted to on-screen text and spoken aloud; a separate module converts speech back to text.

Hand tracking

MediaPipe's hand landmark model provided 21 tracked points per hand in real time — from the wrist through each fingertip — giving enough spatial detail to distinguish between visually similar gestures. These landmark coordinates, rather than raw pixels, became the input features for every downstream model, which kept the system lighter and less sensitive to lighting or background than a pure image-classification approach.

Model training

Four models were trained and compared on the landmark dataset:

  • Random Forest — an ensemble of decision trees, used as the primary classifier for its balance of accuracy and stability.
  • AdaBoost — combined multiple weak classifiers into a stronger one by focusing on previously misclassified examples.
  • Gradient Boosting — iteratively corrected the errors of prior models to improve overall performance.
  • Hybrid Stacking Classifier — a meta-model layered on top of the above, blending their predictions for the final output.

Supporting experiments also compared SVM and K-Nearest Neighbors as alternative classifiers during evaluation.

Evaluation

Each model was tested on a held-out split of the gesture dataset and scored primarily on accuracy (correctly classified instances over total instances) and precision (true positives over true positives plus false positives). A confusion matrix was generated for the Random Forest classifier across an 11-gesture vocabulary, showing strong, concentrated accuracy along the diagonal with only a small number of cross-class misclassifications — confirming the landmark-based feature approach generalized well to unseen samples.

Output: text and speech

Once a gesture was classified, two lightweight modules handled the human-facing side of the system:

  • Text-to-speech — built with pyttsx3, an offline speech synthesis library, converting recognized gesture text into spoken audio with adjustable rate and volume.
  • Speech-to-text — built with the speech_recognition library, capturing microphone input, filtering ambient noise, and transcribing speech via Google's speech API.
# Simplified speech-to-text flow recognizer = sr.Recognizer() with sr.Microphone() as source: recognizer.adjust_for_ambient_noise(source) audio = recognizer.listen(source) text = recognizer.recognize_google(audio)

Outcome

The result was a working, bidirectional prototype: a webcam feed could be turned into live on-screen text and spoken words, gesture by gesture, with the ensemble/stacking approach giving noticeably more stable classification than any single model alone. It's a practical demonstration of combining classical ML ensembles with real-time computer vision to solve an accessibility problem.
